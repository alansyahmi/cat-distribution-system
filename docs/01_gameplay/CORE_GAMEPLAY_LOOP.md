# CaDS — Core Gameplay Loop

- **Document ID:** DOC-GAME-001
- **Version:** 0.1
- **Status:** Draft

## 1. Moment-to-moment loop

**Observe → Interpret → Move → Interact → Read the Response**

The player notices a stimulus or opportunity, interprets what it might mean, navigates the environment, attempts an action and reads the world's response.

Examples:
- Hear a bowl being placed down, investigate, approach carefully and eat if permitted.
- Notice a high shelf, find a climbable route and reach a resting perch.
- Observe a familiar human, approach using a preferred distance and attempt contact.
- See another cat near a passage, assess its posture and decide whether to approach, wait or retreat.

The player should not need a quest marker for every meaningful action. Environment, animation, sound and prior knowledge communicate opportunities.

## 2. Session loop

A typical 15–20 minute session may contain:
1. Start from a familiar safe place or current location.
2. Notice a need, routine, rumour, stimulus or curiosity.
3. Choose a route through the neighbourhood.
4. Encounter objects, people, animals or obstacles.
5. Improvise one or more interactions.
6. Experience immediate and possibly delayed consequences.
7. Gain a discovery, resource, relationship change or new opportunity.
8. Decide whether to continue exploring, rest, return or pursue a new lead.

This is a rhythm, not a mandatory mission sequence.

## 3. Long-term loop

**Explore → Learn → Connect → Establish a Life → Reconsider**

- **Explore:** discover routes, resources, territories and routines.
- **Learn:** understand how the neighbourhood and its inhabitants behave.
- **Connect:** build or damage relationships through repeated encounters.
- **Establish a Life:** develop reliable access to food, shelter, companionship and comfort.
- **Reconsider:** choose how much to rely on people, other animals, familiar territory or independence.

Long-term progress should alter options and world response, not simply increase a level number.

## 4. Needs as prompts, not chores

Needs should create direction and vulnerability without turning play into constant meter maintenance.

For the first prototype, hunger and energy are candidates:
- Hunger creates a reason to seek food.
- Energy creates a reason to rest and makes activity pacing meaningful.

Needs must have generous, testable tuning and clear feedback. They should not decay so quickly that exploration becomes repetitive maintenance. Additional needs such as thirst, comfort, safety, cleanliness or health require separate justification.

## 5. Emergent story pattern

**Situation → Player action → World reaction → Changed conditions → New opportunity**

Example: the player takes food from a shop's outdoor bowl. A shopkeeper notices and becomes wary. The player later returns when the shopkeeper is absent, or earns trust through repeated calm encounters. The shop's behaviour changes, creating a different food opportunity and a small personal story.

The same underlying systems should support different outcomes based on context. This example is illustrative, not a required quest script.

## 6. Narrative and sandbox relationship

Authored narrative introduces memorable characters and tensions, provides optional direction, reacts to important systemic state where feasible, and avoids requiring one exact sequence for every player.

Sandbox systems create meaningful activity between authored beats, affect access and relationships, and preserve continuity. A narrative beat should not reset a relationship without explanation; a systemic simulation should not make a critical story character unavailable indefinitely without recovery design.

## 7. Player feedback loop

Every consequential action should communicate what the cat attempted; whether it succeeded, failed or was interrupted; the most relevant reason; what changed; and what the player can reasonably try next.

Feedback can be diegetic: body language, movement, vocalisation, object animation, sound, NPC response and environmental change. UI text should clarify rather than replace these signals.

## 8. Loop health criteria

The loop is working when players can identify plausible actions without quest markers; needs encourage choices but do not dominate; the same action can produce different understandable outcomes; relationships and discoveries change later opportunities; players can recover from small mistakes; and a short session produces at least one memorable self-directed moment.
